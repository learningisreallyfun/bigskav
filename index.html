Task 3

Abiotic factors

Key non-living components shape the rainforest's structure and productivity.

  • WaterHeavy rain, rivers, and coastal fog
    Hoh: ~140–167 inches/year. Fog supplies moisture during dry summer spells.
  • TemperatureMild climate
    Cool summers, mild winters; reduced freeze frequency helps evergreen fungi and epiphytes.
  • Soil & nutrientsDeep organic layers
    Nurse logs and fungal networks store and cycle nutrients.
  • LightCanopy-controlled
    Low understory light promotes shade-adapted plants like ferns and mosses.
Task 4

Biotic factors

Producers, consumers, and decomposers form complex networks — see the food web below.

Producers (examples)

  • Sitka spruce
    Tall canopy tree; anchors many food chains.
  • Western hemlock
    Shade-tolerant conifer; seedlings on nurse logs.
  • Western redcedar
    Shelter and food for many animals.
  • Sword fern
    Ground cover and forage for elk and deer.

Consumers & others

  • Roosevelt elk — herbivore
  • Black-tailed deer — herbivore
  • Banana slug — detritivore/herbivore
  • Bobcat — predator
  • Northern spotted owl — threatened predator
  • Cougar — apex predator

Task 7

Water, nitrogen, and carbon cycles

Switch between cycles below; they crossfade smoothly for a clearer storytelling experience.

Blue arrows
  1. Abundant rain and fog feed soil and streams; Hoh receives ~140–167 inches/year.
  2. Trees and plants uptake water; transpiration sustains fog and local climate patterns.
  3. Excess water runs into rivers, the ocean, and returns via evaporation.
Orange arrows
  1. Soil bacteria and alder symbionts fix nitrogen into bioavailable forms.
  2. Plants absorb nitrogen; herbivores concentrate and redistribute it.
  3. Decomposition returns nitrogen to soil or N2 via microbial processes.
Light blue arrows
  1. Photosynthesis locks atmospheric CO2 into wood and biomass.
  2. Respiration and decomposition return CO2 to the atmosphere; long-lived trees store carbon for centuries.
  3. Salmon transport marine carbon into streams and forests when they spawn and die.
Task 8

Population — northern spotted owl

  • Status
    Federally threatened
  • 1990s estimate
    ~230 pairs
  • Later change
    Down >80% from 1995–2017
  • Limiting factor
    Competition with barred owl; loss of old-growth.
  • Conservation
    Protect old-growth, manage barred owl competition.
Task 9 · green arrows

Community interactions

  • Mutualism
    Lichen (fungus + alga) — both benefit.
  • Commensalism
    Epiphytes on Sitka spruce.
  • Parasitism
    Ticks on elk.
  • Predation
    Cougar hunts elk.
Task 10

Keystone species

Pacific salmon

Coho, Chinook, and other salmon move marine nutrients into rivers and riparian forests. Their decline reduces food for bears, eagles, and the nutrient subsidy that supports forest productivity.

Task 11

Human impact

Logging old-growth around the park
How it hurts

Removes nest trees, fragments habitat, reduces carbon storage, and affects stream shading and salmon habitat.

Sustainable option

Protect old trees, enforce riparian buffers, favor selective harvests over clear-cuts.
